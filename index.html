Little word meanings

What it meansSixteen little words, each split into the meanings it has in many phrasal verbs, such as UP means finished. A verb can be in two of these if it has two meanings; it then shows the meaning that fits first, and its other meanings straight underneath, on a pale blue background. Meanings that fit none of these are under Other at the end. The meanings, and which verb goes under which, are judgement.

Inside a meaning, very common verbs come first. The other little words come after these sixteen, with their verbs but no meanings yet.

Little words list

What it meansThe verbs grouped by their little word, such as all the verbs with up.

Where it goes

What it meansWith many phrasal verbs, a short word like it, them, me or him must go between the verb and the little word: put it down, not put down it. A longer word can go in either place: put down your bag and put your bag down are both correct.

Where you see itThe meanings where this matters end with Word order: a ✓ next to the right way and a ✗ next to the wrong way. The short word matches the example sentence where it can.

Where you do not see itMeanings with no object, like break down or get up. Verbs where the object always goes at the end, like look after them. Verbs with three words, like put up with.

    6. Learning approach

    Learners can come to the verbs in several ways: by verb family, by little word, by everyday topic, by how important they are, or A to Z. Any of these works, and learners can use whichever suits them.

    I recommend starting with Little word meanings. The little word is not random. It often keeps the same meaning across many verbs: UP can mean finished in eat up, use up and fill up. Many of these meanings grow out of the word’s physical meaning, such as up as a direction. A learner who knows the pattern can make a good guess at a verb they have not met, and has somewhere to put each new verb. A list learned one verb at a time gives them neither.

    Research behind this

    • Rudzka-Ostyn, B. (2003). Word Power: Phrasal Verbs and Compounds. A Cognitive Approach. Berlin: Mouton de Gruyter. The approach the app follows: each little word has a physical core meaning, and its other meanings extend from it. Read a review of the book
    • Kövecses, Z. and Szabó, P. (1996). Idioms: a view from cognitive semantics. Applied Linguistics 17(3), 326–355. Learners who were taught the meanings of up and down did better on a gap-fill of phrasal verbs with up and down.
    • Yasuda, S. (2010). Learning phrasal verbs through conceptual metaphors: a case of Japanese EFL learners. TESOL Quarterly 44(2), 250–273. Learners taught the meanings behind the little words chose the right little word significantly more often than learners taught in the usual way.
    • Boers, F. (2000). Metaphor awareness and vocabulary retention. Applied Linguistics 21(4), 553–571. Grouping expressions by meaning can help learners remember them. In one test with new multi-word verbs, though, the taught group did no better. Boers concluded that it depends on how clear the verb’s meaning is, the learner’s first language and their level.

    So the approach helps most where the meaning of the verb clearly comes from the little word. Verbs under Other on the Little word meanings screen fit no pattern and still need learning one by one.

    7. Sources and judgement

    Sources

    Two published sources were used, both for choosing which first verbs matter most. Nothing in the app is copied from them.

    • Gardner, D. and Davies, M. (2007). Pointing out frequent phrasal verbs: a corpus-based analysis. TESOL Quarterly 41(2), 339–359. Used for the 20 first verbs that are most common in phrasal verbs in the British National Corpus: go, come, take, get, set and 15 more. Together they make 53.7% of the phrasal verbs counted there. Read the article
    • Kovács, É. (2002). Properties of verbs which constitute phrasal verbs. Eger Journal of English Studies 3. Used for the 11 verbs that make the most phrasal verbs in the Collins COBUILD Dictionary of Phrasal Verbs: put, go, come, get, push, pull, take, bring, turn, look and fall. These are the 11 biggest verb families in the app. Read the article

    Corpus

    One corpus stands behind the sources. No corpus was searched directly to make this app.

    • British National Corpus Consortium (2000). British National Corpus: World Edition. Oxford: Humanities Computing Unit of Oxford University. 100 million words of British English. Gardner and Davies took their counts from it.
    • Collins COBUILD Dictionary of Phrasal Verbs (1995), as cited by Kovács. This is a dictionary, not a corpus. Kovács took her counts from it. Collins builds its COBUILD dictionaries on its own corpus, the Bank of English. Open question: this has not been confirmed for the 1995 edition.

    Judgement

    Everything below is judgement. None of it comes from a published list or a measured count.

    • Which phrasal verbs are in the app, apart from the 11 verb families taken from Kovács.
    • The meanings and the example sentences. Claude wrote them. They have not been checked against a published dictionary.
    • The three groups: Very common verbs, Common verbs and Less common verbs, and how common each meaning is.
    • The everyday topics, and which verbs are in each topic.
    • The order of the little words.
    • Which meanings show Word order, and which short word each one uses.
    • The little word meanings, which verb meaning goes under which little word meaning, and the order of the little words on that screen. These follow the cognitive-linguistics approach of Rudzka-Ostyn (Word Power: Phrasal Verbs and Compounds, 2003); the sorting of each verb is Claude's. Read a review of the book

    Two things are not judgement: the size of each verb family, which is a count of the verbs in this app, and the A to Z order.

    What are phrasal verbs?

    A phrasal verb is a verb and a little word that go together: get up, turn off, look after. Together they have a new meaning.

    A new meaning

    You often cannot guess the meaning from the two words.

    lookuse your eyes. Look at the board.

    look upfind information. I looked up the word in the dictionary.

    look aftertake care of. She looks after her dad.

    Some phrasal verbs have three words: put up with (accept something bad), look forward to (be happy about something in the future).

    Why learn them?

    People use phrasal verbs all the time when they speak English. They are in films, songs, work and shops.

    There is often a longer word with the same meaning. The longer word is more formal. It is good for writing and forms. The phrasal verb is good for speaking.

    Speakingfind out · put off · come back · go up

    Writing and formsdiscover · postpone · return · increase

    The little word helps you

    The little word often has the same meaning in many phrasal verbs. UP can mean finished: eat up, use up, fill up. OFF can mean stop: turn off, switch off. If you know the little word, you can often guess a new verb.

    To see this, choose Little word meanings on the home screen.

    One verb, many meanings

    Some phrasal verbs have more than one meaning. Pick up can mean lift (Pick up your bag), collect (I pick up my son at 3) or learn (She picked up English quickly). Look at the sentence to find the meaning.

    Where does it go?

    With many phrasal verbs, a short word like it or them goes in the middle.

    ✓ Turn it off. ✗ Turn off it.

    ✓ Turn off the light. ✓ Turn the light off.

    In this app, Word order shows you where it goes.

    1. The best way to learn is to look at the little words.
    2. At first, focus on the Very common verbs.
    3. Tap a verb to see its meaning and an example.
    4. Search for a verb, or for a meaning like delay.
    5. Try Test what you know to practise.
